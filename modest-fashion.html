Finding modest clothes should not require searching every rack and every website.

The problem

Suitable pieces may exist, but shoppers still have to find them across stores and sites, assess missing details, and imagine how to wear them.

What I did

I studied how shoppers define coverage, where they look for clothes, and what makes suitable options difficult to find. I translated those findings into a proposed shopping assistant and information architecture.

Current outcome A proposed agentic AI shopping assistant, feature map, data-flow diagram, and information architecture. The concept has not been tested or launched.

01 / Where the question began

A problem I kept seeing close to home.

My sister and people around me had difficulty finding modest clothes they liked. I began by asking whether the options were missing because few people wanted them, and whether modest clothing was uncommon in India.

02 / Literature review

A wider lens on modest fashion.

Before defining a solution, I reviewed writing on modest fashion and clothing choice. Much of the research I collected focuses on Muslim consumers, while the wider reading also pointed to comfort, fit, culture, and personal preference. I treated modest fashion as individual clothing preferences rather than one identity or one fixed silhouette.

Theme A

Reasons for dressing modestly differ by context

The reviewed papers discuss faith alongside social expectations, safety, and the wish to keep a modern personal style. I could not assume one motivation for every shopper.

Theme B

Physical shopping still matters, but searching takes work

Fit and comfort give shoppers reasons to visit stores. The literature also describes the effort of finding and assembling suitable pieces across the physical shopping journey.

Selected reading on my research board

Advancing Muslim Modest Fashion Clothing: Impact on Consumer Behavior and Challenges for Young Female Muslim Influencers · Fashion innovativeness in India: shopping behaviour, clothing evaluation and fashion information sources · A Fine Balance: Negotiating fashion and respectable femininity in middle-class Hyderabad, India

Gap 1 · the direction I took forward

Research shows that modest fashion is a growing market and that consumers have different reasons for choosing more-covered clothing. However, there is limited research on how mainstream Indian fashion retailers currently cater to these consumers, particularly through product assortment, organisation, and accessibility in physical stores.

Side note / An important distinction

What “modest clothing” means here

Some of the reviewed papers may consider these forms of modest dressing:

  • salwar kameez
  • kurtas
  • dupattas
  • longer traditional silhouettes
  • clothing considered socially appropriate in that cultural context

In this project, I mean clothing that offers greater body coverage through:

  • longer hemlines
  • full or long sleeves
  • higher necklines
  • less revealing cuts
  • non-cropped tops
  • fuller or looser silhouettes, where preferred
  • layer-friendly designs

The definition is not tied to a religion, culture, or one silhouette.

03 / Narrowing the focus

Who was I researching for?

I narrowed this project to younger shoppers looking for contemporary, more-covered clothing, including everyday and workwear. The focus was on the garment features and shopping experience they wanted, rather than assuming that “modest” meant traditional or faith-based clothing.

The later survey sample was concentrated among 18–25-year-olds and students; it should not be read as representative of all shoppers.

05 / What the research revealed

The difficulty was often finding and assessing what was there.

01 / What coverage means

More than one silhouette

Respondents described coverage through garment features and chose more-covered clothes for overlapping reasons. Among 40 valid responses, 85% selected personal comfort or preference and 60% selected religion or spirituality.

02 / Where they shop

Across channels and sections

Mainstream stores were the most common single shopping option in the survey. When asked where they find suitable clothes in stores, 12 respondents chose regular women’s sections and 12 chose pieces scattered across sections; only 2 chose a dedicated section.

03 / Why it is difficult

Discovery and variety

17 of 40 rated the search difficult or very difficult. A lack of a designated section and limited style variety were the two most reported challenges, echoed by the store audits.

What the store audits added

Potentially suitable pieces could be scattered across sections. A promising garment might still have a lower neckline, shorter length, thin fabric, or need another layer. A garment existing is different from a shopper being able to find and confidently choose it.

The research reframe

I began with: “Is there enough modest clothing?”

When suitable clothing exists, how easily can shoppers find it, assess it against their own preferences, and put an outfit together?

09 / Initial idea exploration

Five ways I considered helping shoppers.

These were directions I explored, not tested concepts.

  1. 01

    In-store kiosk

    Find preferred pieces in one store. Limited to physical shopping and dependent on retailer investment.

  2. 02

    Retailer product aggregation

    Bring products from mainstream retailers into one searchable place.

  3. 03

    In-store mobile app

    Kiosk-like help on a shopper’s phone, dependent on accurate inventory and product-location information.

  4. 04

    Retailer tagging system

    Make coverage attributes easier to find, but require adoption and consistent tagging across retailer systems.

  5. 05

    Combined shopping assistant

    Bring cross-retailer search and store discovery together so the system does more of the search work.

The retailer-data dependency remains unresolved. Choosing AI does not create access to missing product or branch information.

10 / Proposed concept

An agentic AI shopping assistant, with the shopper in control.

01

Tell it what matters

Set sleeve length, neckline, top length, back coverage, opacity, fit, fabric, style, size, budget, and location. Mark must-haves and flexible preferences. Budget stays strict unless the shopper opts into alternatives.

02

Review explained matches

See the source, price, supported details, uncertain details, and relevant return information. Best matches support all required preferences; possible matches explain compromises or show a “Needs checking” tag on the card.

03

Decide what to do

Save, compare, request a participating branch check, or continue to the retailer’s product page. The shopper makes the final decision.

For nearby stores

A participating branch could respond available, unavailable, or unable to confirm to a product, size, and colour request. A pending state appears while waiting. “Available” means checked, not reserved.

For online products

The shopper can follow a clear source link to the retailer’s product page. Unverified details, including opacity, stay marked as uncertain; reviews are labelled as shopper reports.

11 / Saved finds and outfit building

Dress-up games for real clothes.

The shopper can combine saved finds, a photo of something they already own, and other real products. They can explore optional layers, see relevant prices, and save an outfit. This helps with the mental work of imagining combinations.

Saved finds+Owned piece
optional photo+Other real products→Saved outfit

Visual try-on, occasion planning, a full digital wardrobe, and tailoring requests are ideas to assess for scope, not established core features.

12 / How the proposed system would work

Useful answers depend on useful data.

Shopper preferences→Product search & assessment→Explained results→Shopper action

Information in

Retailer product pages and available store data feed product details into search and assessment. Missing or unverified data remains visible. Saved items can feed the outfit builder.

Request and response

A physical-store availability request goes to participating store staff. Their response returns to the shopper as available, unavailable, or unable to confirm.

Can the concept access sufficiently useful product information from each retailer?

How can a participating branch respond reliably to availability requests?

14 / What remains to validate

The concept gives me sharper questions to test.

  • Do shoppers understand must-have and flexible preferences and the match explanations?
  • Do uncertainty labels help shoppers make decisions they trust?
  • Is a store availability request useful to shoppers and feasible for branches?
  • Does the outfit builder help people decide what to keep, pair, or buy?
