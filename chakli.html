Redesigning the
Traditional Chakli Maker

A festive kitchen tool that shouldn’t hurt the hands that use it.

Role
Ergonomic Analysis and Research, Concept Developement
Timeline
3 weeks, 2026
Team
4 designers
Methods
Interviews · RULA · REBA · SI · ART · SCAMPER
One-hand, side-lever operation

The problem

Traditional chakli presses need high force and repeated wrist twisting. Most users felt wrist pain and fatigue within 20–30 minutes.

What we did

Interviews and observation with 10 users, a product study of 5 existing presses, an ergonomic audit (RULA, REBA, SI, ART), then 5 concepts → 1 final design.

Where it landed

A ratchet-driven, stand-mounted chakli maker worked by a side lever, taking the twist out of the wrist and the load off the fingers.

01 — Why this

A festive ritual that ends in sore wrists

Every Diwali we watched our mothers and grandmothers spend hours at the chakli press — and stop every few batches to rub their wrists. A tool meant to create joy shouldn’t be a source of pain.

“My wrist feels a sharp pain after only 10 minutes of twisting.”— Participant, user interview

05 — Design principles

Six rules the redesign had to follow

  1. 01Design for minimum effort, not maximum strength
  2. 02Spread force across the palm, not the fingertips
  3. 03Stay stable under high resistance
  4. 04Keep the wrist in a neutral posture
  5. 05Grip securely with oily, doughy hands
  6. 06Fit a wide range of hands — not an ideal one

06 — Ideation

Using SCAMPER, we pushed past the screw press: levers, ratchets, gravity-assisted plungers, counter-weights, suction bases and quick-release barrels.

08 — Final design

Ratchet-driven, stand-
mounted, pressed with one hand

Ratchet mechanism

Step-by-step motion cuts the force needed on each press.

Side lever

Pressed with four fingers or the palm — no wrist twisting.

Vertical A-frame stand

Takes the load off the arms and keeps the press steady.

Clear window

Shows how much dough is left, so batches aren’t interrupted.

10 — Reflection

What I’d change next

Trade-offs

— The tall vertical stand takes up more kitchen storage space.

— We did not design for the post usage part of it

Next steps

— Build a working prototype and test with at least 10 users, including elderly users.

— Re-run RULA and REBA on the prototype.

— Explore a foldable or wall-mounted stand.

What I learned

— Measuring strain (not just asking about it) made the case for every design decision.

— Small ergonomic changes to everyday objects can make a meaningful difference in how they are used
